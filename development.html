Personal Development

Building habits for a stronger future

A reflection on George S. Clason’s The Richest Man in Babylon and how its lessons on saving, judgment, and self-development can inform my life as a student.

Read the book PDF

Chapter reflection

Seven Cures for a Lean Purse

Arkad’s lessons describe wealth as a set of repeated choices: keep some of what I earn, plan spending, make considered use of savings, and keep improving my ability to earn.

01

Pay myself first

The chapter begins with setting aside a portion of what I earn before the rest is spent. I can apply this by saving a small, realistic amount from any allowance or income and treating that transfer as part of my plan, not as whatever happens to be left.

02

Plan my spending

Clason distinguishes needs from wants and shows how spending can expand to absorb income. As a student, I can write down regular expenses, identify purchases that do not matter much to me, and make room for school costs and goals I value.

03

Put savings to work

Saving is presented as a beginning; the next step is to make saved money productive. For me, this means learning how saving and investing work before acting, starting with knowledge and patience rather than expecting quick returns.

04

Protect what I build

The story of Arkad’s early loss warns against trusting money to someone without the right experience. I can carry this into adulthood by checking claims, understanding risk, asking qualified people questions, and being cautious with offers that promise easy profit.

05

Choose a stable foundation

The book’s advice about owning a home reflects its setting and its concern for security. I read the broader lesson as making thoughtful choices about shelter and long-term costs. I should not rush into a major commitment; I should learn, compare options, and decide when it fits my circumstances.

06

Prepare for the future

Clason asks readers to prepare for later life and for people who depend on them. Even as a student, I can begin with small habits: keeping an emergency buffer when possible, learning about financial planning, and thinking beyond immediate wants.

07

Grow my ability to earn

This cure shifts the focus from money to the person earning it. It connects closely to my studies: improving my software skills, practicing consistently, completing projects, and learning from feedback can increase the value I bring to future work.

Chapter reflection

The Five Laws of Gold

In the story of Nomasir, the laws are tested through both success and failure. The chapter emphasizes that keeping wealth depends on knowledge and judgment as much as on earning it.

01

Save consistently

The first law returns to the habit of reserving part of my earnings. I take this as a reminder that a modest, regular start can matter more than waiting until I have a large amount to save.

02

Give savings a purpose

Money can grow when it is put to suitable use. Before I invest, I need to understand the options and the time involved. I should see growth as gradual and uncertain, not automatic.

03

Protect against avoidable loss

The third law values careful guidance. I can apply it by learning from reliable, qualified sources and asking questions before making financial decisions, rather than relying on confidence alone.

04

Understand before investing

The fourth law warns against putting money into ventures I do not understand. This is a useful check on impulse: if I cannot explain how an opportunity works and what could go wrong, I am not ready to commit money to it.

05

Be skeptical of impossible returns

The final law rejects schemes that promise extraordinary rewards without credible explanation. I should be patient, verify information, and remember that a claim of guaranteed fast wealth is a reason to pause, not a reason to hurry.

My biggest takeaway

Small, informed actions compound

The strongest lesson I take from these chapters is that financial progress is not one lucky event; it is a practice. Saving regularly, making deliberate choices, protecting what I have, and building my skills all require discipline over time. As a student, I can begin before I have a full-time income: I can learn to budget, save what is realistic, research before deciding, and invest in my education. The book’s examples come from another era, so I would not follow every recommendation literally. I would carry forward its habits of patience, preparation, and continued learning.

Reference

Clason, George S. The Richest Man in Babylon. Signet, 1988 edition. “Seven Cures for a Lean Purse,” pp. 25–41; “The Five Laws of Gold,” pp. 65–73.
